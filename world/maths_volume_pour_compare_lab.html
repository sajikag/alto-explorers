Volume of Solids — Pour & Compare Lab

Recreate the textbook's own sand-pouring activities: fill a pyramid or cone with sand and empty it into a matching cuboid or cylinder — watch how many pours it takes to fill it completely, and see Archimedes' sphere-cylinder-cone relationship in action.

Takeaway: Fill the square-based pyramid with sand and empty it into a cuboid with the same square base and the same height. Do this exactly three times and the cuboid fills up completely, with nothing left over. That is why the volume of a pyramid is exactly one third of the volume of the matching cuboid: V = ⅓ × base area × height.

Takeaway: A cone and a cylinder that share the same base radius and the same height follow the very same 1 : 3 relationship as the pyramid and cuboid. Pour the cone's sand into the cylinder three times and the cylinder fills exactly: V = ⅓πr²h for the cone. (This lab uses π ≈ 22/7, as in the textbook.)

Takeaway: Archimedes' idea: fit a sphere snugly inside a cylinder of the same radius, with height equal to the sphere's diameter (h = 2r) — the sphere just touches the top, bottom and sides. The sand that fits in the gaps around the sphere exactly fills a cone of that same radius and height. In other words: Volume of cylinder = Volume of sphere + Volume of cone — the classic way the formula V = 4⁄3πr³ for a sphere is derived.
